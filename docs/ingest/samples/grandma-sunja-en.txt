[00:00] Interviewer: Let us start at the beginning. Where were you born?
[00:03] Grandma Sunja: Um, I was born in Busan, in a little house near the harbour, I think it was nineteen forty-six, in the spring. My mother always said the fishing boats were coming in the morning I was born, so there was a lot of noise, you know, and the midwife could hardly hear herself think.
[00:25] Interviewer: Tell me about your father.
[00:28] Grandma Sunja: My father repaired boats. He had big hands, really big, and he did not talk much at all. He would come home smelling of tar and sawdust, and he would sit by the door and take his boots off, and that was it, that was the whole conversation for the evening. But on Sundays he took me to the pier and we watched the ships. I loved those Sundays.
[00:55] Interviewer: And your mother?
[00:58] Grandma Sunja: My mother sold fish at the market. She left before dawn and came home after sunset every single day, and her hands smelled of salt. She was the one who held the family together. She could count money faster than anyone I ever met, and she never learned to read, but nobody ever cheated her, not once.
[01:22] Interviewer: Mm-hm.
[01:25] Grandma Sunja: Yes, she was something.
[01:27] Interviewer: What was your house like?
[01:30] Grandma Sunja: We lived in a shack made of scrap boards, and the wind came through the walls in winter. We had one stove for the whole family, so we all slept close to it. There were six of us in two rooms. I remember my brother Jaesung kept a little bird in a box under his bed, and he fed it rice, and it lived for almost two years.
[01:56] Interviewer: Did you go to school?
[01:58] Grandma Sunja: I only finished fifth grade. Um, we could not afford more. But my teacher, Mrs. Han, said I had the most beautiful handwriting in the whole school, and she gave me a pencil box as a prize when I left. I still have that pencil box, it is in the cabinet in my bedroom. It has a picture of a crane on the lid.
[02:23] Interviewer: What happened after you left school?
[02:26] Grandma Sunja: At eighteen I started at a textile factory, and I gave my whole pay to my mother. Every month, the whole envelope. She would put a little aside for me in a tin, and when I got married she gave me the tin, and there was enough in it for a sewing machine. That machine is what I started my own business with, many years later.
[02:52] Interviewer: When did you meet your husband?
[02:54] Grandma Sunja: I met Minho in nineteen sixty-six, at a church supper. He was so nervous that he spilled gravy all over his shirt, and I laughed, and he went red, and that was that. We married in June of nineteen sixty-seven, in a small ceremony, because we did not have money for anything bigger.
[03:17] Interviewer: Where did you live after you married?
[03:20] Grandma Sunja: We moved to Daegu, because Minho had found work at a printing house there. The pay was better than the harbour. We rented two rooms above a tailor's shop, and the tailor, Mr. Baek, became like an uncle to us. He taught me to cut cloth, and that is how I started sewing for other people.
